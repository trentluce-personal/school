## Capacity planning

Capacity is the upper limit or ceiling on the load that an operating unit can
handle. The basic questions are:

- What kind of capacity is needed?
- How much is needed?
- When is it needed?

## Importance of capacity decisions

1. Impacts ability to meet future demands.
2. Affects operating costs.
3. Major determinant of initial costs.
4. Involves long-term commitment.
5. Affects competitiveness.
6. Affects ease of management.
7. Globalization adds complexity.
8. Impacts long-range planning.

## Capacity measures

- Design capacity: maximum output rate or service capacity an operation, process,
  or facility is designed for.
- Effective capacity: design capacity minus allowances such as personal time,
  maintenance, and scrap.
- Actual output: rate of output actually achieved. The slide states that it
  cannot exceed effective capacity.

## Efficiency and utilization

Both measures are expressed as percentages:

```text
Efficiency = (Actual output / Effective capacity) × 100%
Utilization = (Actual output / Design capacity) × 100%
```

## Determinants of capacity

- Facilities
- Product and service factors
- Process factors
- Human factors
- Operational factors
- Supply chain factors
- External factors

## Capacity issues

- Design flexibility into systems.
- Take stage of life cycle into account.
- Take a "big picture" approach.
- Prepare to deal with capacity "chunks."
- Attempt to smooth out capacity requirements.
- Identify the optimal operating level.

## Break-even point (BEP) analysis

The original includes image-only break-even diagrams. Available text labels are

and "Total cost," with an origin of 0. See the

### Assumptions

- One product is involved.
- Everything produced can be sold.
- Variable cost per unit is the same regardless of volume.
- Fixed costs do not change with volume.
- Revenue per unit is constant with volume.
- Revenue per unit exceeds variable cost per unit.

## Make or buy (outsource)

Consider available capacity, expertise, quality considerations, nature of demand,
cost, and risk.

## Economies and diseconomies of scale

- Economies of scale: if the output rate is less than the optimal level,
  increasing output rate decreases average unit costs.
- Diseconomies of scale: if the output rate is more than the optimal level,
  increasing output rate increases average unit costs.

## Evaluating alternatives

Production units have an optimal rate of output for minimal cost. Minimum cost
and optimal operating rate are functions of the size of the production unit.

The original graphs plot average cost per unit against rate of output, identify
minimum cost and minimum average cost per unit, and compare small, medium, and
large plants. The curves remain in the original.

## Service capacity

- Need to be near customers: capacity and location are closely tied.
- Inability to store services: capacity must match the timing of demand.
- Degree of volatility of demand: peak demand periods.
